Great Molasses Flood

The Great Molasses Flood, also known as the Boston Molasses Disaster, was a disaster that occurred on January 15, 1919, in the North End neighborhood of Boston, Massachusetts.

A large storage tank filled with approximately 2.3 million U.S. gallons of molasses burst, sending a large wave of molasses through the surrounding streets. The disaster killed 21 people and injured approximately 150 others.

Background

Molasses can be fermented to produce ethanol, which was used in alcoholic beverages and was also an important component in munitions. The disaster occurred in Boston's North End at the Purity Distilling Company facility at 529 Commercial Street near Keany Square.

A considerable amount of molasses was stored at the facility. The Commercial Street tank was located next to Boston Harbor and was used to receive molasses from ships and store it before the material was transferred by pipeline to the Purity ethanol plant in nearby Cambridge.

The tank stood approximately 50 feet tall and 90 feet in diameter. It could contain as much as 2.3 million U.S. gallons of molasses.

Flood

On January 15, 1919, temperatures in Boston had risen above 40 degrees Fahrenheit (4 degrees Celsius), climbing rapidly from the frigid temperatures of the preceding days. On the previous day, a ship had delivered a fresh load of molasses, which had been warmed to decrease its viscosity for transfer.

Possibly due to the thermal expansion of the older, colder molasses already inside the tank, the tank burst open and collapsed at approximately 12:30 p.m. Witnesses reported that they felt the ground shake and heard a roar as it collapsed. Others described a tremendous crashing sound, a deep growling, and a sound like a machine gun as the rivets shot out of the tank.

The density of molasses is about 1.4 metric tons per cubic meter, making it approximately 40 percent denser than water. The collapse released a large amount of potential energy. The resulting wave of molasses reached approximately 25 feet high at its peak and moved at an estimated 35 miles per hour.

The wave was powerful enough to drive steel panels from the burst tank against the girders of the adjacent Boston Elevated Railway's Atlantic Avenue structure and temporarily tip a streetcar from the railway's tracks. Nearby buildings were swept from their foundations and crushed. Several blocks were flooded to a depth of approximately 2 to 3 feet.

Stephen Puleo quotes a report from The Boston Post:

Molasses, waist deep, covered the street and swirled and bubbled about the wreckage. Here and there struggled a form; whether it was an animal or human being was impossible to tell. Only an upheaval, a thrashing about in the sticky mass, showed where any life was. Horses died like so many flies.

Aftermath

First on the scene were 116 cadets under the direction of Commander H. J. Mellish, who stepped off USS Nantucket, a training ship of the Massachusetts Nautical School. They ran several blocks toward the accident and entered the knee-deep molasses to pull out victims, while others kept bystanders back.

Shortly after, Boston police, Red Cross workers, Army personnel, and Navy personnel arrived. Rescue efforts continued for several days. Many victims were so covered in molasses that they were unrecognizable. The rescue workers worked through the thick molasses, which had hardened in the cold air, making search operations exceptionally difficult.

Causes

Several factors contributed to the disaster. In the days following the accident, an investigation pointed toward structural failure due to poor construction, inadequate inspection, and unseasonably warm weather causing internal pressure.

The tank had been built hastily and tested inadequately. When filled to capacity, the steel used was too thin and lacked sufficient fatigue resistance to hold the load.

Area Today

Today, the site of the Purity Distilling Company tank is occupied by a complex of recreational fields known as Puopolo Park and Langone Park. A small plaque at the entrance to the park commemorates the disaster.

In Popular Culture

The flood has inspired numerous books, television episodes, and musical compositions, becoming a staple of New England folklore and historical trivia.
