The Great Molasses Flood

Boston, Massachusetts — January 15, 1919

The Great Molasses Flood, also known as the Boston Molasses Disaster, occurred in Boston's North End on January 15, 1919.

Background

The disaster occurred at the Purity Distilling Company facility on Commercial Street in Boston's North End. The company stored large quantities of molasses in a massive steel tank near Boston Harbor.

The tank was approximately 50 feet tall and 90 feet in diameter and could hold approximately 2.3 million gallons of molasses. Molasses was valuable because it could be processed into ethanol and was also associated with industrial production during World War I.

Flood

On January 15, 1919, temperatures in Boston rose rapidly after several unusually cold days. A new shipment of warmer molasses had recently been added to the tank.

At approximately 12:30 p.m., the tank suddenly failed. Millions of gallons of molasses were released and moved through the surrounding streets with enormous force.

The resulting wave reached several meters in height and damaged buildings, vehicles, and the nearby elevated railway structure. The flood spread through several blocks of the neighborhood.

The physical behavior of the molasses contributed to the disaster. As the material spread and cooled, it became increasingly difficult for people and animals caught in the flood to escape.

Aftermath

Rescue efforts began almost immediately. Cadets from a nearby training ship reached the scene, followed by Boston police, Red Cross workers, and military personnel. Rescuers searched through the molasses and debris for survivors.

Cleanup

Cleanup was difficult because the molasses had spread through streets, buildings, transportation facilities, and other parts of Boston. Workers used water and other materials to remove the sticky substance.

The cleanup effort continued for weeks, while traces of molasses were carried throughout the surrounding area by people, vehicles, and rescue workers.

Fatalities

Twenty-one people died as a result of the disaster and approximately 150 people were injured. Several horses also died. Victims included workers, residents, and children in the surrounding neighborhood.

The disaster affected the North End community both immediately and for years afterward.

Causes

Investigations after the disaster examined the design and construction of the tank as well as the conditions that existed when it failed.

Investigators found evidence of structural weaknesses, inadequate testing, and problems with the materials used to construct the tank.

Later engineering studies have examined the tank using modern scientific methods. These studies have helped researchers understand how the structure failed and why the molasses moved through the neighborhood so rapidly.

Area Today

The property where the tank once stood was eventually redeveloped. The former industrial site is now associated with public recreation in Boston's North End.

The disaster remains part of Boston's local history. Memorials and historical markers commemorate the people who died and the impact the flood had on the neighborhood.

The event also became significant in the history of engineering and construction regulation. Changes in oversight and professional review followed the disaster.

In Popular Culture

The Great Molasses Flood has continued to appear in books, music, journalism, and other forms of popular culture. Its unusual circumstances have helped keep the event familiar to later generations.

The disaster has also been the subject of historical writing and scientific investigations that examine both the human experience and the engineering problems behind the tank's failure.
