Above 14,000 feet

Big mountains. Better preparation.

Colorado's fourteeners are mountains whose summits rise above 14,000 feet. They offer incredible views, demanding climbs, and a reason to learn about weather, terrain, and responsible outdoor recreation before leaving the trailhead.

Whether you are working toward your first summit or comparing routes for a future trip, use this guide to start planning.

What makes a fourteener different?

Altitude changes the experience

At high elevations, there is less oxygen available with each breath. A familiar trail can feel much harder when it climbs above tree line, where shade and shelter become limited. Fitness helps, but it does not eliminate the risks associated with altitude.

The summit is only half the journey

Hikers need enough daylight, energy, water, and weather awareness to return safely. Reaching the top is never more important than making a good decision about when to turn around.

landscape A useful fact

Colorado has more than 50 commonly recognized fourteeners. The exact count depends on the criteria used to distinguish independent summits from nearby subsidiary peaks.

Planning principle: Choose a route based on conditions and experience, not elevation alone.

Compare popular Colorado fourteeners

These well-known routes illustrate how distance and terrain vary. Round-trip distances are approximate and can differ by route, trailhead, and navigation choices.

Selected Colorado fourteeners: summit elevation and approximate round-trip hiking distance
Mountain Elevation (feet) Approximate round-trip distance General route character
Mount Bierstadt 14,060 About 7 miles Popular hiking route with a defined trail for much of the climb.
Grays Peak 14,278 About 8 miles Steep hiking terrain; often combined with nearby Torreys Peak.
Torreys Peak 14,275 About 8–9 miles Often climbed as part of the Grays and Torreys combination.
Mount Yale 14,196 About 9.5 miles Sustained uphill hiking with increasingly rocky terrain.
Longs Peak 14,259 About 14–15 miles A long, committing alpine route with significant exposure and scrambling.

These figures are planning estimates, not current trail reports. Confirm the route, trailhead access, and conditions before your trip. Difficulty depends on weather, fitness, altitude response, and experience.

Prepare before leaving home

Check the mountain forecast

Mountain weather can change rapidly. Thunderstorms, high winds, snow, and low visibility can make exposed terrain dangerous. Check an appropriate mountain forecast before leaving and reassess conditions throughout the day.

Pack for changing conditions

  • Carry enough water and food for the expected trip and delays.
  • Bring insulating layers and rain protection.
  • Pack a map, navigation device, and backup power as appropriate.
  • Carry a headlamp, first-aid supplies, and emergency essentials.
  • Wear footwear suited to the terrain and current conditions.
  • Tell someone your route and expected return time.

schedule Set a turnaround time

Decide in advance when you will head back, even if you have not reached the summit. Turn around earlier if storms approach, visibility deteriorates, someone feels unwell, or progress is slower than expected.

Reliable resources for your trip

Use official land-management information and current forecasts instead of relying only on old trip reports.

  • 14ers.com — route descriptions, trailhead information, and hiking community resources.
  • National Weather Service — weather forecasts and alerts.
  • USDA Forest Service — information about public lands, closures, and recreation areas.
  • National Park Service — park alerts, regulations, and safety information for applicable areas.

Always verify the managing agency for your specific route, since access rules and closures vary by location.
